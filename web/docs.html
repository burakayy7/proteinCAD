How to use proteinCAD

A protein viewer you can design in. Load a structure or a cryo-EM map, pick a patch on a surface and ask a model to build something that binds there — or turn one part of an assembly against another and see what the interface does.

Three parts, and only one of them needs anything behind it. The viewer runs entirely in your browser: structures, density maps, styles, selections, measuring, export. So does the Motion tab, which scans how a rotor packs against its axle through a full turn. Both work right here on the website, with nothing installed and no account. Design is the part that sends jobs to a GPU, so it needs a server behind it and, on the website, a sign-in. If you only want to look at things, start at loading structures and stop at Designing proteins.

Getting started

Open the viewer and click one of the example buttons. 1CRN is crambin, 46 residues, small enough to see all at once. Drag to rotate it, scroll to zoom, click a residue to select it. That is most of the interface.

The window has three parts: the structures list on the left, the viewport in the middle, and a panel on the right with three tabs — Inspect, Design and Motion. Either side panel collapses with [ and ], which is worth knowing on a laptop.

Loading structures

Four ways in, and they all end up in the same place:

  • Open… in the toolbar, for files on your machine. Pick several at once if you want.
  • Drag and drop a file anywhere on the window.
  • The id box — type 4HHB and press Fetch. It takes EMDB ids as well, which is how you load a density map.
  • The example buttons on the empty screen.

.pdb, .ent, .cif and .mmcif all work. mmCIF is usually the better choice for anything large, since PDB files can't hold more than 99,999 atoms.

You can also put an id in the URL, which is handy for links:

index.html?load=6WG3
index.html?load=EMD-25575

Loading more than one structure is normal and expected — that is how you compare two things, or dock one against another. They all share the viewport and each gets a row in the left panel.

Big structures are fine. The 335,000-atom flagellar motor (7CGO) is one of the examples specifically so you can see what the viewer does under load. It takes a few seconds to parse. Surfaces get coarser automatically on anything that size, because a fine one would take minutes.

Density maps

Type an EMDB id where you would type a PDB id — EMD-25575 — and press Fetch. The viewer downloads the cryo-EM map, contours it, and adds it to the structures panel underneath the structures. One of the example buttons loads that entry, a designed protein rotor, so you can try it without knowing an id.

The box works out which databank you mean from the id itself. Four characters is always a PDB entry. An EMDB id either carries its prefix (EMD-25575, emd_25575) or is a bare number of five or six digits (25575).

A map's row has a colour swatch, ✥ to put the move handle on it, ◉ to hide or show it, and ✕ to remove it. Click the arrow at the left of the row to open it:

levelThe contour level, as a slider. The readout gives it in the map's own units and in σ.
EMDB levelPuts the slider back to the level recorded in the EMDB entry.
Style menuSolid, Wireframe or Transparent. Solid is slightly see-through on purpose: the usual reason to have a map and a model in one scene is to see the model inside the map.
fittedA button for each atomic model deposited against the map, which loads it into the same scene. Only shown when the entry has one; the example map does not.

The open row also gives the map's title, its grid size and voxel spacing, the reported resolution, and how many triangles the surface took.

Contour level

The level is the control that matters. A map is a block of numbers, and the surface you see is wherever those numbers cross one value. Set it too low and you get a solid brick of noise; too high and there is nothing to draw.

So a map opens at the level its depositors recommended in the EMDB entry, or at three σ above the mean when the entry does not give one. Drag the slider and the surface follows. The σ figure beside the level is there because raw map units differ by orders of magnitude from one map to the next, and σ is the scale that means the same thing on all of them.

Maps are reduced as they download. Anything over about eight million voxels is averaged down in blocks until it fits, and the row says when that has happened — 400×400×400, 1.05 Å/voxel, shown at 200×200×200. Averaging keeps the noise from turning into speckle on the surface, but detail finer than the displayed spacing is not there to see.

A map is scenery, not a molecule. Clicking it selects nothing, the selection language does not see it, and Export leaves it out, because a PDB file has nowhere to put one. Nothing here fits a model into a map either: you can slide a map against a model by hand with ✥, but there is no refinement and no correlation score.

Maps come from EMDB by id only. There is no way yet to open a map file from your own disk.

Display options

Under Inspect → Display:

  • Waters and Hydrogens — both off by default. Waters in particular will bury a structure in dots if you turn them on without thinking.
  • Depth cue fades distant parts into the background. It helps more than it sounds like it will on anything crowded.
  • Hover info puts whatever is under the cursor in the status bar at the bottom.
  • Background — any colour. White is usually what you want for a figure.
  • Clip cuts away the front of the scene so you can see inside. Drag the slider to cut deeper. Good for looking into a channel or a pocket.

Selecting things

Clicking picks a residue. The modifiers change what "a click" means:

ClickThat residue, replacing the selection
Shift + clickAdd it to the selection
Alt + clickThe whole chain
Cmd/Ctrl + clickJust the single atom
EscClear the selection
Cmd/Ctrl + ASelect everything

What's selected is highlighted, counted in the status bar, and broken down in Inspect → Selection — how many atoms, which chains, which residues, the composition, the radius of gyration. Two useful things you can do with a selection:

  • I isolates it: hides everything else so you can see what you picked without the rest in the way. Shift+H brings it all back.
  • Backspace hides the chains the selection touches.

For an assembly, Inspect → Components is usually the faster way in. It lists the distinct molecules rather than the chains — a handful of proteins for the flagellar motor, instead of 219 rows. Click one to select every copy of it, Shift-click to add another, double-click to select and frame it.

The selection language

Clicking gets tedious past about ten residues. The box at the top of Inspect → Selection takes an expression instead. If you've used PyMOL this will feel familiar.

chain A and resi 10-45
protein and not water
byres (within 4.5 of ligand)
ss h or ss e
name CA and b > 70

Combine terms with and, or, not and parentheses. Every keyword:

Short forms exist where they're obvious: i for resi, r for resn, e for elem, & and | for and/or.

byres (within 4.5 of ligand) is the one to remember. It gives you the binding pocket around whatever is bound — every residue with an atom inside 4.5 Å of the ligand, whole rather than clipped. Isolate that with I and switch to sticks and you're looking at the site.

The structures panel

Every file you load gets a row on the left, with its chains underneath. Click a row to make that structure active — the tools that act on one structure act on that one. Per row you can:

  • Hide or show it, or any single chain.
  • Recolour a chain by clicking its colour swatch.
  • Set a style for just that structure, overriding the toolbar. This is how you get a surface on one thing and cartoon on another.
  • Duplicate it, which is the starting point for building an assembly by hand.
  • Remove it.

Density maps get rows of their own below the structures, with a different set of controls — see Density maps.

Measuring

Press M or click Measure in the Inspect panel, then click two atoms. The distance in ångströms appears in the list and a label appears in the viewport. Keep clicking pairs to add more. Measurements follow the atoms if you move a structure, so you can watch a distance change as you dock something by hand.

Clear removes them all; the ✕ on a row removes one. Press M again to get back to normal selection.

Moving parts around

Inspect → Move / rotate. Pick a target first, then a tool.

Targets: Structure moves the whole active one, Chain moves just the selected chain, Volume moves the design volume (see below).

Tools: Move (G) and Rotate (T) put a handle on the target — drag an arrow or a ring. The numeric fields below show the position and rotation and can be typed into if you want something exact. Reset puts everything back where the file had it.

Holding Shift with G or T forces the whole structure even when a chain is selected.

Ring of N

Down at the bottom: Ring of n about an axis. This copies the active structure evenly around that axis, which builds a Cn ring in one click — a pore, a symmetric assembly, a scaffold. Duplicate adds a single copy instead. The copies are real structures in the list and can be moved, hidden and exported like anything else.

Saving your work

  • Snapshot saves a PNG of the viewport exactly as it looks.
  • Export writes the whole scene as a PDB file, including everything you moved, duplicated or arrayed — positions as they are now, not as they were in the file.

Export writes PDB, which has hard limits the format can't get past: 99,999 atoms and single-character chain ids. Anything over that is renumbered and remapped on the way out. For a really large assembly, export is lossy about names, not about coordinates.

Two things Export does not do. It leaves density maps out, because the format cannot hold one. And it does not undo a turn made in the Motion tab: a rotor left at 140° is written at 140°. Press Reset on the landscape first if you want the pose as loaded.

Turning an assembly

Some assemblies are two parts on one axis: a ring threaded on an axle, a rotor inside a stator. The question worth asking about one of those is what the interface does as one part turns against the other. The Motion tab answers it. It rotates one component about the shared symmetry axis in fixed steps, scores how the two parts pack at each angle, and draws the result as a curve in a strip under the viewport.

The shape of that curve is the result. Flat means the rotor turns freely. Wells mean it has preferred orientations and has to climb out of them. Wells whose two sides differ in height mean it leaves more easily one way than the other, which is what a ratchet is.

The scan runs in your browser. Nothing is uploaded and no account is needed, on the website or anywhere else.

1. Say which part is which

Load the assembly and open Motion. Under Rotor and axle:

  • Guess from molecules takes the two largest molecules in the active structure and makes the one with more copies the rotor. For a deposited two-component assembly that is usually right, and it is one click.
  • From selection, on the Rotor row or the Axle row, uses the chains you have selected. Alt + click selects a chain; clicking a molecule under Inspect → Components selects every copy of it at once.
  • Clear forgets both.

Each row shows which chains it holds. A chain cannot be both — assign it to one part and it leaves the other — and both parts have to be chains of the same structure. Changing either one discards the current curve, since it was measured for different parts.

You do not set the axis. It is measured from each part's own symmetry, by superposing a chain onto its symmetry mates, so an assembly that was not deposited along z still scans about the right line.

2. Scan

stepHow finely the turn is sampled, from 45° (8 points) down to 2° (180 points). The default is 5°, which is 72.
score byWhat scores each angle. packing score — here is the one that runs, "here" meaning this browser.
rise ±Greyed out. It would also slide the rotor along the axis for a two-dimensional scan, which the in-browser scorer does not do.

Press Scan. A whole turn usually takes about a second, and the curve draws itself as the angles arrive. Stop ends a scan early and keeps what was drawn. The line under the buttons says how far it got and how long it took.

3. Drag the curve

The cursor on the plot and the rotor in the viewport are the same control. Drag along the curve and the rotor turns, so a well at 140° is a shape you can look at rather than a number.

Drag on the plotTurns the rotor to the nearest angle that was scanned. It snaps, so the numbers shown are always measurements and never interpolations.
The slider underneathTurns it to any whole degree.
⟨ and ⟩Previous and next scanned angle
⟨⟨ and ⟩⟩Previous and next deep well
ResetBack to the orientation as loaded
×Hides the strip. Scanning again brings it back.

On the plot itself, the yellow dots are deep wells and the smaller grey ones are lesser minima. The faint dashed lines are one period apart, which is what makes "eight wells, 45° apart" something you can see. The curve is dashed while a scan is still running. Below it is the readout for the angle under the cursor: the score, the buried area in Å², the number of clashing atom pairs and the gap across the interface.

Turning never damages anything. Every angle is applied to the pose the scan started from rather than on top of the last one, so Reset returns the exact coordinates you loaded. Measurements follow the rotor, so a distance across the interface can be watched through the turn.

Reading the numbers

When a scan finishes, What the curve says appears in the Motion tab. Hover any line for what it means.

symmetryThe rotational fold measured for each part, such as rotor C4, axle C8.
axis agreeThe angle between the axis the rotor implies and the one the axle implies. Close to zero is what you want. A large figure means the parts do not share an axis, and the curve is not measuring what it appears to.
periodThe spacing at which the curve repeats, measured from the curve.
symmetry saysWhat the period has to be: 360° over the lowest common multiple of the two folds. ✓ means the scan agrees and ✕ means it does not; "too coarse to check" means this step size cannot test it.
wellsHow many minima, split into deep and lesser. A well is deep when its prominence is at least a tenth of the curve's range.
barrierThe mean height to climb out of a well, going forwards.
depth rangeHighest point minus lowest.
asymmetryForward barrier against reverse, from −1 to 1. Zero is a rotor equally happy to turn either way. A dash means it cannot be measured: with one well per period, both directions cross the same peak.
as loadedWhere the orientation you loaded ranks among those scanned, such as rank 1 of 72, in a well. For an experimental structure it should be at or near the bottom.

Pick a step that divides the period. Symmetry fixes the period before anything is scored: a four-fold rotor on an eight-fold axle has to repeat every 45°. A scan can only confirm that if 45° is a whole number of steps. At 10° it is not, so the panel says "too coarse to check" and names a step that works — 15° in this case. That is the scan declining to test the period. It is not the assembly failing the test.

What the score is, and is not

The packing score is geometry. It combines the surface area buried between the two parts, how much their atoms overlap, and the gap across the interface into one number in arbitrary units, where lower means better packed. It finds the orientations that fit together well, which is most of what gives a landscape its shape.

It is not a force field, and its numbers are not binding energies. The period of a landscape is guaranteed by symmetry and will be right whatever scores it. The depths of the wells carry no such guarantee — they are what this scorer says, and should be read as "packs better than" rather than as energies.

If the panel says only a few percent of the turn is clash-free, the two parts are interdigitated: they mesh rather than rotate, and most of the curve is a steric wall. That is a finding about the assembly, not a fault in the scan.

Running proteinCAD from its own Python server adds a second entry to the score by menu, interface ddG, reserved for a Rosetta energy function. It is a placeholder in this build and shows as unavailable.

Designing proteins

This is the part that needs the Python server and a GPU. The Design tab will tell you plainly if it can't reach one.

The idea: you pick a patch on a protein, and RFdiffusion draws a new protein backbone against it. That backbone has no sequence — every residue comes back as glycine, because it's a shape and nothing more. So there's a second step, where ProteinMPNN chooses amino acids for it and ESMFold folds that sequence on its own to see whether it comes back to the shape it was designed for.

The two steps are deliberately separate. The first takes minutes and is worth looking at before you pay for the second.

1. Pick a site

Click Pick site, then click residues on the target. Click again to unpick. Or select residues however you like and press From selection.

These are the hotspots — the residues the new chain should touch. Three to six on a contiguous patch works well. Scattered picks across opposite faces give the model an impossible instruction, and you'll get nonsense back.

2. Shape and size (optional)

Draw a cylinder, box or sphere in the scene to say roughly how big the new protein should be and where it should sit. Drag the handles to move and resize it. Use volume converts its volume into a residue count — about 133 Å³ per residue — and fills in the length range for you.

This is a sizing aid, not a constraint. RFdiffusion has no way to be told "fill this box". What actually reaches the model is the length range and the hotspots. If you skip the volume entirely, nothing is lost — just set the length yourself.

3. Choose a protocol

The make menu at the top of Run. Six of them, and they want different things from you:

ProtocolWhat it doesNeeds
BinderBuilds a new chain against the residues you picked. The default, and the only one that uses hotspots.Target + hotspots
Motif scaffoldingKeeps the picked residues exactly as they are and builds a new protein around them. For carrying a functional site into a new scaffold.Target + picked residues
UnconditionalNo target at all. Samples a protein of the given length from nothing. The honest way to see what the model does on its own.Just a length
Symmetric oligomerSamples a symmetric assembly. The length is the whole assembly, so it has to divide by the number of chains.Length + symmetry setting
Partial diffusionVariations on something you already have. Load a design back in, pick it, and it gets noised partway and denoised again. Good for exploring around a hit.A structure to vary
Fold conditionedConditions on a fold described as secondary structure and adjacency rather than coordinates.Scaffold files, see below

Switching protocol rewrites the settings that belonged to the old one and leaves alone anything you changed yourself.

Fold conditioned is offered in full but isn't made easy. It needs _ss.pt and _adj.pt files generated in advance on the GPU machine by RFdiffusion's own helper_scripts/make_secstruc_adj.py. The panel takes the paths; it doesn't make the files.

4. Contigs

The contigs box is the string RFdiffusion actually gets. Leave it empty and one is built for you from what you picked — that's right almost every time, and the line underneath always shows you what it will be.

Edit it when you need something the panel can't express: a chain break, a specific motif layout, an inpainted span. Edit auto copies the generated one into the box so you can change it rather than write it from scratch; Auto puts it back. An edit is never a one-way door.

Roughly, A1-46/0 60-70 means "keep chain A residues 1–46, chain break, then build 60 to 70 new residues".

5. Advanced settings

Everything RFdiffusion can be told, 44 settings in six groups: motif, sampling, model, symmetry, potentials, scaffold. The panel only shows the ones your protocol normally uses — Show all reveals the rest, Reset puts everything back to RFdiffusion's own defaults.

Two live out in the open because they're the ones people actually turn:

  • steps — diffusion steps. 50 is the default. Fewer is faster and rougher.
  • noiseScale — how much randomness. Lower gives more conservative, more designable backbones; higher explores more and fails more.

On/off settings have three states, not two: — means "don't mention it, use RFdiffusion's default", which is different from explicitly sending off. The overrides box at the bottom passes anything you type straight to run_inference.py, one per line, for the rare thing the panel doesn't know about.

If you're not sure whether a setting took effect, run the job and click Command on it in the Jobs list. That's the actual command line the model was run with. It's the only way to tell a setting that was applied from one the GPU worker was too old to have heard of.

6. Run it

Set designs (how many backbones to generate — 4 to 8 is a sensible first go), a seed if you want the run repeatable, and press Run design. Preview spec shows exactly what would be sent, if you want to look before committing.

The job appears in Jobs with a progress count and a line saying what it's doing right now. Each finished design gets a button — click it to load the result into the viewer alongside the target, where it's a structure like any other. You can measure it, colour it, export it.

Jobs keep running if you close the tab. They're on the server, not in the browser.

7. Sequence and fold

A backbone isn't a protein yet. Set the numbers under Sequence and fold — how many sequences to design (seqs, 8 is fine), how many of those to actually fold (fold, 2 by default because folding is the slow part), and the sampling temperature. Then find the finished job in the Jobs list and press a design's number in its sequence row.

When it finishes you get a line like:

✓ 2 of 8 folded · best 1.34 Å from the backbone, pLDDT 87

That's the number that matters. ESMFold saw only the sequence — no coordinates, no backbone. If it comes back within 2 Å of the shape you designed, with pLDDT above 80, the sequence really does encode that shape. That's the standard self-consistency filter and it kills most bad designs.

The folded design will look almost identical to the backbone. That's the point, not a bug. The prediction is superposed onto the backbone it came from, so position and orientation match by construction and only the shape can differ. Looking different is the failure case.

But check which you're looking at. With the defaults only the first two results are folded; the other six are the backbone wearing a different sequence — same coordinates exactly. Folded ones are the highlighted buttons. Hover any result for its numbers, or switch to sticks: a real prediction has side chains, a backbone doesn't.

What this does not tell you is whether your design binds. It folds the new chain on its own, so it measures self-consistency, not affinity. A design that passes this is worth taking further; it isn't a binder yet.

Where the GPU is

The on menu beside Run picks which one. Three possibilities:

mockNo GPU at all. Builds a real helical bundle and puts it where the volume was drawn, so you can exercise the whole workflow offline. It is not a design method — nothing it produces means anything biologically. It exists for testing.
remoteA GPU you started yourself, usually a Colab notebook with a tunnel. Set the address under Compute endpoint. A Colab tunnel gets a new address every session, and that box is where you follow it without restarting anything.
ec2A machine on AWS that's created when somebody needs it and destroyed when nobody does. On the version at adumbra.burakayy.com this is the only option, and it's already selected.

With ec2 you get a GPU section showing the state. The instance is kept stopped, because a GPU bills by the second whether or not it's computing. The first job that needs it starts it — about a minute or two — and it stops itself once nothing has needed it for fifteen minutes. The countdown is right there, along with a Stop now button.

Pressing Start while you're still setting a design up means even the first job runs immediately. That's the only way to avoid the wait.

The shared machine

The copy on the website works differently, and it's worth understanding because it explains a wait you'll otherwise find puzzling.

There is no machine most of the time. Not a stopped one — none. That's the point: a GPU costs about fifty cents an hour whether or not it's computing, and one sitting idle overnight for nobody is the most expensive thing a project like this can do. So the machine is created when somebody wants it and destroyed thirty minutes after the last job.

Which means the first design of a session has real steps to get through, and the GPU machine section shows them happening rather than hiding them:

Start GPU machineAsks AWS for one. Four or five minutes to exist, boot, and pull the model software — the panel says which stage it's on.
Download beside each modelFetches that model's weights onto it — RFdiffusion is 3.9 GB, ESMFold 8.5 GB, ProteinMPNN a tenth of that. Half a minute each. The bar shows the megabytes.

Run design lights up when the machine is ready and the models that protocol needs are there. Until then it tells you what's left — "Start the GPU machine, then download RFdiffusion."

The sequence buttons need different weights, ProteinMPNN and ESMFold, and there's no point making you fetch eight gigabytes before you've drawn a backbone. So they are never greyed out. Press one before those models are on the machine and it tells you what is missing and starts downloading it, with the progress showing in the GPU machine section. It does not then run on its own: press it again once the models show ready. If the machine itself is not running, it says that instead.

You don't have to press any of it. Pressing Run with nothing downloaded works fine — the job just spends that time first instead, and says "fetching the models this job needs" while it does. The buttons exist so you can get the waiting out of the way while you're still setting a design up.

Three things follow from the machine being shared:

  • It's already warm if somebody else started it. The panel shows ready, the models show ready, and you skip straight to Run.
  • You can't kill it, but you can retire it. There is no Stop button and no Terminate — on a machine other people are using, either is a way to end somebody else's run, and nothing in the account has the permission to do it. Retire asks instead: the machine finishes anything running or queued, then shuts itself down, and the next design starts a fresh one. Press it twice, because everyone shares the machine. It also goes on its own after thirty idle minutes.
  • Starting it again is a fresh download. Nothing survives, because nothing exists. That's the trade for it costing about a dollar a month rather than seventeen.

Occasionally you'll see "AWS has no g4dn.xlarge to spare in any of the three zones". That one is exactly what it says — the region is full — and it usually clears in a few minutes. Press Start again.

Signing in

If you're running proteinCAD yourself — python3 -m proteincad, or a Colab notebook — there's no sign-in and no Account section. It's your machine and your GPU.

The copy on the website is different, because the GPU behind it costs about fifty cents an hour and somebody is paying for it. So:

  • Looking at structures needs no account. Loading files, styles, colours, selections, measuring, export — all of it works signed out. So do density maps and the Motion tab, which run in your browser. That's most of the tool.
  • Sending a design needs one, and anyone can make one. Click Create account in the Account section. Give an email address and a password; a code arrives by email; type it back in. The account doesn't work until you do, so use an address you can read.
  • Already have one? Sign in is the other button. Either way you come back to where you were, with whatever you had loaded still loaded.

Once you're in, the Account section shows how many jobs you've used today. There are three limits, and they exist because every one of those jobs is GPU time:

20 a dayper person. Resets at midnight UTC — the panel says how long that is.
2 at oncequeued or running. Wait for one, or cancel it.
100 a dayacross everybody together.

A sequence-and-fold run counts the same as a backbone run, because it uses the same card for about as long.

Starting the GPU machine is counted too — five a day each, twenty across everyone. A machine that starts and runs nothing still bills for its idle minutes, which is what that limit is for.

Two smaller differences on the shared copy. Structures come straight from the RCSB, and maps straight from EMDB, rather than through the server, so Fetch works exactly the same but nothing is cached for you. And finished designs download through a link that's good for five minutes — if you leave a job list open for an hour and click Load, refresh the list first.

Signing out clears everything on your device. Closing the tab does the same thing on its own.

When something breaks

What you seeWhat it is
"No server answering on api/health"You're on the static site, or the Python server isn't running. The viewer works; design doesn't. Start it with python3 -m proteincad.
"This server is running code older than the files on disk"The web files reload on every request, the Python process doesn't. Restart the server.
"Pick at least one target residue"The protocol you chose needs hotspots and none are picked. Press Pick site and click some.
A job fails immediatelyOpen Command on it. The message from the model is usually specific — a bad contig, a length that doesn't divide by the symmetry, a missing scaffold file.
The tunnel stopped resolvingColab session ended. Re-open the tunnel in the notebook, then paste the new address under Compute endpoint.
Surface takes foreverIt's a big structure. Use Backbone trace to navigate, then isolate a region (I) before switching to surface.
A structure appears to be missing atomsOnly the first model of an NMR ensemble is shown, and alternate locations past the first are dropped.
"EMDB has no entry EMD-…"There is no map with that number. Check the digits against the entry's page on EMDB.
"Nothing is enclosed at this level"The contour level is above everything in the map. Drag the level down, or press EMDB level.
A map shows as a solid blockThe level is too low, so the surface is wrapping noise. Drag it up, or press EMDB level.
"The download ended early"The connection dropped partway through a map, which leaves a hole in it. Remove the map and fetch it again.
Scan is greyed outHover it for the reason. Usually no rotor or no axle has been chosen yet.
"…does not have two distinct molecules"Guess from molecules needs two different molecules to tell apart. Select the chains of each part yourself and use From selection.
"a landscape is measured within one structure"The rotor and the axle are in two different loaded files. Export the scene, which writes both into one PDB file, load that back in and pick the parts again.
"too coarse to check" beside the periodThis step size cannot test the period symmetry predicts. The note underneath names a step that can.
"This machine is running an older build"The shared GPU machine started before the site was last updated and keeps the code it booted with. Retire it; the next design starts a current one.
"Asked to retire … and still running"The machine is too old to act on a retire. Only whoever runs the deployment can clear it, by terminating the instance in the AWS console.
A local server, but the page acts like the websiteThe checkout holds a web/config.json from a deployment, so the page is calling that API and not your server. Add ?local=1 to the address to ignore it in that tab.

proteinCAD is a viewer with a design loop attached. It shows the asymmetric unit as deposited — mmCIF assembly operators aren't applied — and surfaces are Gaussian rather than solvent-excluded. Large density maps are block-averaged to fit in memory, and the landscape score is a geometric packing measure, not an energy.
